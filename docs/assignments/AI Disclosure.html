Prompt 01

[Attached: IMG_4785.jpeg — photograph of a disassembled in-tank fuel pump hanger assembly laid out on a wooden table: stamped steel top plate with two electrical studs, bail handle, long central tube, mid-span bracket, stamped bottom cradle, the cylindrical pump can with red and black leads, plus a black elastomer cradle collar, a rubber grommet, and two white acetal retainers.]

Is there any way to take this fuel pump apart? The pump itself that is.

Response

Yes, but it's a one-way trip in most cases.

That cylinder is a turbine-style in-tank pump, and both end caps are held on by a rolled/staked crimp in the outer can — the outlet end (black plastic cap with the terminals and check valve) and the inlet end (the black plastic strainer housing). To open it:

  • Work the crimped lip of the can outward with a small screwdriver or a Dremel cutoff wheel run shallow, then pry the end cap free. The outlet end is usually easier since the plastic cap sits proud.
  • Inside you'll find: check valve and pressure relief, brush plate/commutator, armature on a shaft, and a turbine impeller in a two-piece pump chamber at the inlet end.

Practical reality: the crimp doesn't reliably re-form, the end cap O-ring gets nicked, and there are no service parts sold for the internals. If you re-crimp poorly it'll leak internally, lose pressure, or pull air. It's fine for a teardown to see why it failed (worn brushes, scored impeller, debris in the turbine) but not a rebuild path.

If the goal is diagnosis rather than repair, checking current draw and deadhead pressure before cutting tells you most of what's inside.

Purge/ventilate it first — residual gasoline in the housing plus a cutoff wheel is a bad combination.

Prompt 02

Can you explain in detail how this pump would work and be made? Tell me like you are the engineer who made it and explain all of your design decisions in detail. Include the economics and function of each decision you made in the manufacturing process of each part. be thorough and include every part of the design to the real specifics (hanger, means of doing the welds, the pump and its internals, pipe bending, plate forming, etc). Response

I'll answer this as the responsible engineer would, but with one honesty caveat up front: I can't identify the exact part number or application from the photo, so what follows is how a module like this actually gets designed and built in the industry rather than a recitation of one specific OEM's drawing package. Where I give numbers, they're typical values for this class of part, and I'll flag where the range is wide.

Part 1: The requirements I was handed

Before any geometry exists, I get a spec from the vehicle side that looks roughly like this:

  • Deliver 80–130 L/h at 3.0 bar gauge, at 13.5 V, at 20 °C
  • Hold pressure at hot idle with fuel at 60 °C without vapor-locking
  • Draw under 8 A steady, under 25 A inrush at −30 °C
  • 3,000 hours endurance, some of it on dirty fuel
  • Survive 15 years / 240,000 km of tank vibration
  • Fuel-tight and permeation-compliant at the tank interface
  • Not audible in the cabin
  • Fit through a tank aperture of a fixed diameter, and reach the bottom of a tank whose floor is not flat
  • Cost target: a few dollars at the module level in volume

That last one shapes almost every decision below. Automotive fuel pumps are a commodity, and the winning design isn't the cleverest — it's the one that hits the flow curve with the fewest machined surfaces and the fewest secondary operations.

Part 2: The pump cartridge

Why a turbine (regenerative) pump. I have three realistic architectures: gerotor, roller-cell, and regenerative turbine. I picked turbine.

A gerotor or roller-cell pump is a positive-displacement machine. Every rotation traps a fixed volume and shoves it out. That gives excellent efficiency (50%+) and it self-primes well, but it produces a discrete pressure pulse per chamber. At 5,000 rpm with 7 lobes, that's 583 pulses per second — right in the middle of the band the human ear hates, and that energy goes straight into the fuel column, up the delivery line, into the rail, and into the firewall. You end up adding a pulsation damper to fix a problem you created.

The regenerative turbine is different. Fuel enters at the inlet port, gets flung radially outward by a vane, exits into the surrounding annular channel, spirals back inward, and re-enters the next vane a few degrees around. It does this dozens of times over roughly a 300° arc before reaching the outlet. Each pass adds a small increment of head. The pressure rise is therefore continuous and distributed around the arc, not lumped into discrete pulses. The output is smooth enough that I don't need a damper at all.

The tradeoff is efficiency — I'm running maybe 20–30% where a gerotor gives 50%. That costs me a couple of amps, which the alternator absorbs. I traded electrical efficiency for NVH and part count, and for a 3-bar port-injection system that's the right trade every time. It's also why turbine pumps live inside the tank: they're poor at pulling a suction lift, so I submerge them and let gravity prime them.

The impeller. Injection-molded, almost certainly a filled thermoplastic — PPS or a high-temp polyamide with glass fill. The impeller has 30–70 small vane pockets around its rim, and each one has a curved profile that matters to the recirculation pattern. Machining that is absurd. Sintering can do it but with more dimensional scatter. Injection molding gives me the vane geometry for free once the tool exists — the complexity is in the steel, not in the cycle time. Tooling for a multi-cavity impeller mold runs maybe $40–80k; the piece price after that is pennies. At a million units a year the tool is noise.

I also want the impeller to be non-metallic because it's running with tens of microns of clearance to a metal housing. If a hard particle gets in there, I'd much rather the plastic impeller wear or the debris embed itself than have a metal-on-metal seizure that stalls the motor and pops the fuse. The impeller is a loose fit on the shaft with a flat or double-D drive. It floats axially — deliberate, so hydraulic forces let it self-center between the two housing faces.

The pump chamber. Two housing halves sandwiching the impeller, each with the annular channel formed into the face, and a stripper section between outlet and inlet where the channel is closed off to break the recirculation and force flow out the port. These are almost always sintered powder metal, which gives me the channel profile near-net-shape from the compaction tool. But one surface I cannot leave to chance: the flat face that sets the axial clearance to the impeller. That gets ground.

This is the single most important dimension in the pump. Leakage past the impeller face scales steeply with clearance — roughly with the cube of the gap in the laminar regime. Going from 25 µm to 50 µm doesn't halve my efficiency, it craters it. So: the faces get double-disc ground flat and parallel; the impeller thickness is measured on every part; and parts are selectively assembled, with impellers sorted into thickness bins and housings into depth bins so the line pairs a thick impeller with a deep housing.

That selective assembly step is a genuine cost lever. It lets me hold a 20 µm assembly clearance while manufacturing everything to 60 µm tolerances. Holding 20 µm on every individual part would be several times more expensive than the sorting station.

The motor. Permanent-magnet brushed DC, two-pole, running flooded in gasoline. Fuel fills the entire motor cavity and flows through it from pump outlet to the outlet cap. Why that's acceptable and even preferable: (1) Cooling — the fuel carries away I²R and commutation heat, which is also why a pump run dry dies quickly; you've removed its coolant, not just its load. (2) Combustion — the brushes arc, but the interior is far above the upper flammability limit for gasoline vapor. It's essentially all fuel, no oxygen. Nothing to burn. (3) Lubrication — the journal bearings run on fuel.

The rotor is a stack of laminations with skewed slots, which smears out cogging torque and reduces the torque ripple that would otherwise show up as an audible whine. Magnets are ferrite arc segments bonded into the can — not rare-earth. Ferrite is a tenth the cost and I have plenty of volume to work with.

Commutator material is a real decision, not a default. Copper is cheaper and has lower contact drop. But if the market includes ethanol blends, gasoline becomes electrically conductive, and you get galvanic and electro-erosion attack on the copper bars — the commutator literally dissolves. So for E10-and-up markets I specify a carbon commutator paired with resin-bonded graphite brushes. It costs more and has higher contact drop, which costs me flow, but it's the difference between a 3,000-hour life and a 300-hour life in Brazil.

Bearings are sintered bronze or carbon-graphite journal bushings, one in each end cap, fuel-lubricated. No seals, no grease — grease would wash out in minutes.

The outlet cap does four jobs: a check valve (ball on a light spring) that holds residual pressure so a hot restart doesn't crank forever; a pressure relief valve opening around 7–9 bar so a kinked line doesn't stall and cook the motor; RFI suppression via choke coils and usually a capacitor, which is a homologation requirement under CISPR 25, not optional; and the blade terminal interface.

Assembling the can. The outer shell is a deep-drawn or rolled-and-seam-welded steel tube. The end caps are inserted and the can lip is rolled inward on a spin-crimp station. Crimping is a two-second operation with no consumables, no heat, and no cleanup. Threading requires machining; welding near a plastic cap and bonded magnets is asking for trouble. The crimp also lets me apply a controlled axial preload through the stack, which is how I set the bearing and clearance stack-up. It's also why you can't put it back together — the crimp is a one-way plastic deformation and there are no service parts for the internals.

Every single pump gets 100% end-of-line tested: flow at nominal pressure, current draw, leak check, and often a noise/vibration signature check. Not sampled — every one. The warranty cost of a field failure dwarfs the test cost.

Part 3: The hanger module

The top plate is progressive-die stamped from cold-rolled steel, roughly 1.5–2.0 mm. In one die, running at multiple strokes per second, the strip gets blanked, the central dish drawn, the mounting holes pierced, the perimeter tabs formed and bent, and the part cut off. The dished shape adds stiffness — the plate sees tank pressure and vacuum cycling and a flat disc that size would oil-can — and gives a defined register for the sealing gasket and lock ring.

Why steel and not molded plastic? On a modern high-volume car it would be molded plastic with an integrated flange and reservoir. Steel here says either an older design, a lower volume where a $150k mold tool can't amortize, or an application where the ground path and sealing method favor metal. Steel gets me an electrical ground and a static-dissipation path for free.

The electrical feedthrough — the two studs in the white insulator — is the hardest single component in the module. It has to pass current through a fuel-tight, permeation-tight barrier, survive fuel on one side and road salt on the other, and hold that seal for 15 years across thousands of thermal cycles. There are two sealing interfaces, terminal-to-insulator and insulator-to-plate, and both leak if you get them wrong. Typical construction is a brass terminal post insert-molded into a glass-filled thermoplastic or thermoset insulator, staked or crimped into the plate with an interference seal. Higher-end designs use a genuine glass-to-metal seal. This part gets its own validation plan: helium leak testing, thermal shock cycling, torque-retention on the nut.

The tube is welded steel, roughly 6–10 mm OD with a 0.7–0.9 mm wall, bent on a CNC rotary-draw bender with an internal mandrel. The mandrel supports the bore through the bend; without it, anything tighter than about 2× diameter collapses the inside wall and wrinkles. The thing nobody expects is springback — steel tube bent to 90° relaxes to maybe 87°, and the amount depends on the exact heat and wall thickness of the coil you're running, so you bend first articles, measure, and load the correction into the program. When a new steel lot comes in, you re-verify. Tube ends get flared or bead-rolled so a clamped hose can't walk off under pressure and vibration.

Joining. The joints aren't all made the same way, and the sequence is the design. Brackets and tabs to tube: resistance projection welding — fast, no filler, no atmosphere control, a fraction of a second per joint, perfect for structural joints that don't need to be leak-tight. Tube through the plate: brazing, not welding, because the joint has to be gas-tight for the life of the vehicle and welding thin-wall tube to a thicker plate risks burn-through and distortion.

For brazing, the high-volume answer is copper furnace brazing in a continuous mesh-belt furnace. Copper paste or a ring is placed at each joint, the assembly is fixtured, and it runs through a reducing atmosphere at around 1,100 °C. The copper melts and wicks into the joint by capillary action — every brazed joint done in one pass with no operator skill involved. Lower volumes use induction brazing with silver alloy, one joint at a time. The critical sequencing consequence: braze before plating. Any plating would burn off or contaminate the joint in the furnace. Process order is form, weld, braze, clean, plate.

Plating. That olive-yellow tint reads as either a zinc flake coating (Geomet/Dacromet family) or zinc with a yellow trivalent passivate. The reason I'd lean toward zinc flake is hydrogen embrittlement: electroplating drives atomic hydrogen into the steel and on stressed formed parts that can crack them later. Zinc flake is dip-spin-bake with no electrolytic hydrogen at all and gives excellent salt spray hours per micron. Its downside is that it's a coating, not a plate — less uniform on edges and it can be worn through mechanically. For a part nobody touches after installation, that's fine.

The cradle and isolators solve one problem: the pump must not touch metal anywhere. A running pump is a small motor spinning at thousands of rpm with brush chatter and hydraulic noise. If it's rigidly coupled to the hanger, that structure-borne energy goes into the plate, into the tank shell, and the tank becomes a large, thin, well-coupled loudspeaker a meter from the rear passengers' ears. Everything about the mounting is a low-pass filter. The pump sits in a molded elastomer cradle boot, the outlet spigot goes into the tube through a rubber grommet, and the lower end sits in a plastic retainer. Nothing is metal-to-metal.

Elastomer selection is nitrile for gasoline-only markets, fluoroelastomer for aggressive or alcohol-containing fuels, durometer around 50–60 Shore A. One design detail that catches people out: fuel-swollen dimensions, not dry dimensions. These elastomers swell 10–20% in gasoline, so the parts are designed at their swollen state, which means a fresh dry part often feels slightly loose. That's correct, not a defect.

The white plastic pieces are almost certainly acetal (POM): excellent fuel resistance, low moisture absorption, low creep, easy to mold with snap features. Nylon would be cheaper but it absorbs water and alcohol and grows dimensionally, which I don't want on a retention feature.

Grounding and static. Fuel flowing through the system generates static charge by contact electrification. In a plastic-heavy module you have to design a deliberate conductive path. Here the brazed steel path to the plate to the tank does that for me as a byproduct, which is one of the quiet advantages of a metal hanger.

Part 4: Validation before I release the drawing

  • Endurance: 1,000–3,000 hours at rated point, a portion on fuel dosed with standardized test dust (ISO 12103-1 A2/A4)
  • Hot fuel handling: fuel at 60–70 °C, verifying no cavitation collapse of the flow curve
  • Cold crank: −30 to −40 °C, confirming inrush doesn't exceed the fuse or drop system voltage below relay dropout
  • Sour/aged fuel soak: peroxide-dosed and alcohol-blended reference fuels (CE10, CM15)
  • Vibration: random PSD profile representing the tank-mounted environment, plus drop and shock
  • Salt spray on the external side; permeation on the module
  • EMC per CISPR 25
  • Burst and pressure cycling on the tube and brazed joints

Part 5: Where the money actually goes

The pump cartridge is the majority of the module cost, and within it the biggest single line items are the ground housing faces and the motor's copper and magnets — not the impeller, not the can. On the hanger side, the dominant costs are the electrical feedthrough (low volume, high validation burden, sole-source), the brazing operation (furnace time and energy per part), and the plating (outsourced, priced by weight and rack time). The stampings and the tube are almost free by comparison — steel plus seconds of press time.

Which is exactly why the design looks the way it does. Every place I could push work into a tool instead of a machining operation, I did. Every place I could replace a tight tolerance with a sorting operation, I did. And the two things I refused to cheapen — the ground clearance faces and the sealed feedthrough — are the two things that would come back as warranty claims.

If you do end up cutting the crimp open, the things worth looking at are the impeller vane edges (rounded or chipped means abrasive wear), the housing faces (scoring means debris), and the brush length versus the spring travel. Those three tell you almost the whole failure story.

Response

[Two diagrams generated. Diagram 1 — vertical cutaway of the pump cartridge showing, bottom to top: inlet strainer, turbine stage, flooded motor, brushes and commutator, and outlet cap with check valve, with flow arrows running up the sides through the motor. Diagram 2 — face-on view of the turbine stage: impeller disc with vane pockets around its rim, the annular channel, inlet and outlet ports adjacent to one another, the stripper block between them, and small loop arrows showing fuel spiralling out into the channel and back in.]

Why "regenerative": the fuel doesn't pass through the pump once. It gets re-used — regenerated — by the same set of vanes dozens of times on its way around.

Here's the sequence for one small parcel of fuel: (1) It enters the inlet port and falls into a vane pocket on the spinning impeller rim. (2) Centrifugal force throws it outward into the ring-shaped channel machined into the housing. (3) In the channel it's moving slower than the impeller, so it drifts backward relative to the rim — and gets scooped up by the next vane pocket a few degrees behind. (4) That vane flings it outward again. And again. And again.

The path is a helix: it corkscrews around the ring while the ring itself carries it toward the outlet. Each individual toss adds only a tiny bit of energy — but the parcel makes something like 50–100 of them across roughly 300° of travel. The pressure builds up like a staircase, one small step at a time.

Contrast that with a gerotor or roller-cell pump. Those trap a fixed slug of fuel in a chamber and squeeze it out — all the pressure rise happens in one violent event, once per chamber per revolution. Big pressure gain per pass, but a hard pulse each time. The turbine has the opposite character: tiny gain per pass, but hundreds of passes, all overlapping at different points around the ring. The output pressure is nearly a flat line. That's the whole reason it's in your tank — it's a quiet pump, and quiet matters more than efficiency when the pump is bolted to a large sheet-metal tank a meter from someone's ears.

The stripper is the piece people miss. If the channel were a continuous unbroken ring, fuel would just keep circling forever and never leave. So there's a short section where the channel is closed off, right between the outlet and inlet ports. Fuel spiralling around hits that wall and has nowhere to go but out the outlet port. The stripper is what turns a circulating loop into an actual pump.

Two consequences worth knowing. First, this is why turbine pumps hate air: they don't grip vapor well enough to fling it, so a bubble breaks the chain of tosses and pressure collapses. They're poor self-primers, which is exactly why the pump is submerged in the tank rather than mounted on the frame rail. Second, this is why the face clearance is everything: the gap between the vane rim and the channel is 20–40 microns of axial clearance on each side. Pressurized fuel leaking backward across that gap short-circuits all the work the vanes just did. Open it up to 60 microns and the pump barely makes pressure. That's the dimension that gets ground and selectively assembled, and it's why a worn pump makes noise but no flow.

Prompt 04

show me the impeller specifically and how it forces fluid to move in the direction it does.

Response

[Two diagrams generated. Diagram 1 — radial cross-section through the impeller rim, showing scooped vane pockets on both faces of the disc, each facing a semicircular channel in the stationary housing, with circulating vortex arrows in each side channel and the rotation axis marked as out of the page. Diagram 2 — the rim "unrolled" flat: a row of vane pockets on the moving impeller below a stationary channel, with a scalloped path showing fuel looping from one pocket into the channel and down into the next pocket, plus two velocity arrows showing the impeller rim moving faster than the fuel.]

The vanes are buckets, not blades. That's the first thing to unlearn. On a centrifugal pump, the impeller has long curved blades and the fluid rides one of them from the eye all the way to the discharge — a single, complete acceleration. On this impeller, each pocket is a stubby little scoop maybe 3–4 mm across, and any given parcel of fuel only stays in one for a few degrees of rotation before it's ejected. There's no aerodynamic subtlety to the shape because there's no time for any.

Two pushes, at right angles to each other. Tangential: the trailing wall of the pocket is a solid surface moving sideways at maybe 15–20 m/s. It hits the fuel in the pocket and shoves it in the direction of rotation. Plain momentum transfer, like a paddle. This is the push that carries fuel around the ring toward the outlet, and it's the direct answer to your question — the fuel goes that way because a wall is physically moving that way and pushing on it. Radial: the moment that fuel starts rotating with the impeller, it has angular momentum and wants to leave. Centrifugal force throws it up and out of the pocket into the channel.

Neither one alone would work. Tangential push without the radial escape would just drag fuel in a circle at rim speed. Radial throw without the tangential push would spray fuel outward and leave it there.

The slip is the mechanism, not a loss. The fuel is always slower than the rim, and it has to be. If fuel in the channel ever reached rim speed, there'd be zero relative motion between the vane and the fluid, and the vane would stop doing work on it — the pressure rise would go to zero. So the fuel is perpetually falling behind. It gets scooped, flung, decelerates in the stationary channel against the housing walls, falls further behind the rim, and gets caught by a vane further back. That lag is what guarantees there's always another scoop coming.

Where the pressure comes from. Each individual toss adds only a few kPa. But the fuel doesn't get to escape — the stripper is blocking the ring, so the only way out is through the outlet port, and everything already stacked up ahead of it is pushing back. So each vane is doing work against a progressively higher back-pressure as the fuel travels around. There's a real pressure gradient around the ring: near the inlet it's basically tank pressure, and by the time you reach the stripper it's 3 bar. Roughly 300 kPa spread over 300 degrees — a smooth ramp, not steps.

Two consequences that fall straight out of this. It can't pump vapor: momentum transfer depends on mass, and gasoline vapor is about 1/700th the density of the liquid, so a vane flinging vapor imparts almost nothing. The parcel doesn't get thrown outward, doesn't rejoin the channel, and the chain breaks at that point in the ring. That's cavitation in a turbine pump — not the erosion kind you get in a propeller, just a dead zone where the staircase stops climbing. It's why the pump is submerged, and why running a tank near empty on a hot day makes the pump noisy.

And direction is set by rotation, not geometry. The pockets are essentially symmetric radial slots. Reverse the motor's polarity and the impeller spins the other way, and the pump does exactly what it did before, only now it draws from the outlet port and pushes toward the inlet. Nothing breaks and nothing stalls; it just runs backwards. Which is why the terminals on that outlet cap are polarity-marked and why a wiring mistake on a fuel pump swap gives you a pump that hums normally and delivers nothing.

Worth flagging one thing before the numbers: I can't confirm from the photo which E30 pump this is, and the E30 has two quite different fuel systems. Two things to check on your part — the top plate having one pipe stub versus two (two = 55 L tank housing, one = 63 L), and whether a level sender board mounts to the housing with four screws (that's the post-9/87 main pump assembly). The assembly in your photo reads as the later single in-tank main pump to me, so that's what I've analyzed.

Factory values

  • System pressure, M20 Motronic (325e/i/is/ix): 3.0 bar above manifold pressure
  • System pressure, 320i / 323i: 2.5 bar
  • Idle reading at rail: ~2.4–2.5 bar (≈36 psi) with ~0.5 bar plenum vacuum
  • Vacuum line off: 3.0 bar (≈43.5 psi) — the pressure test condition
  • Residual after shutdown: must not fall below ~2.0–2.2 bar (check valve test)
  • Deadhead, return blocked: ~5 bar reached readily, below the relief setting
  • Delivery, service minimum (Bentley, inline pump): 0.875 L in 30 s ≈ 105 L/h
  • Delivery at the rail, E30 M3 (S14): 750 cc in 30 s = 90 L/h
  • External main pump, pre-9/87: Bosch 0 580 464 070, BMW 16 14 1 179 232 — 130 L/h at 3.0 bar, 12.8 V, 6.5–7 A, 52 mm dia × ~175 mm, 12 mm inlet / 8 mm outlet
  • In-tank main pump, 9/87 on: BMW 16 14 1 179 992 (VDO/Bosch/Delphi) — 5.2 A nominal, 9 A max
  • In-tank pre-supply pump, pre-9/87: BMW 16 14 1 179 415 (VDO), low pressure only
  • Injectors: Bosch 0 280 150 715, 15.9 Ω, ~149–166 cc/min at 3 bar
  • Tank: 55 L standard, 63 L Touring / facelift M20, saddle type

The injector flow number is the one place sources genuinely disagree — Bosch's own datasheet gives 116.5 g/min on n-heptane, which back-converts to about 166 cc/min, while the E30 community consistently quotes 149–152 cc/min on gasoline. Some of that gap is real (gasoline is denser, so volumetric flow through a fixed orifice at fixed ΔP drops), some is sloppy citation.

The flow budget

Take a 325i at its US rating of 168 hp. At a BSFC of 0.45–0.50 lb/hp·hr, the honest range for a 1980s port-injected naturally aspirated six at peak power: fuel mass flow at redline WOT is 76–84 lb/hr = 34–38 kg/h, which at 0.745 kg/L is 46–51 L/h.

Now the injectors. Six at 150 cc/min gives 54 L/h at 100% duty; at 166 cc/min, 60 L/h. So the injectors are running somewhere between 77% and 95% duty at peak power. That's not a comfortable margin, and it matches what E30 people find empirically — the stock injectors are the first thing that runs out on a mildly built M20.

Against that, the pump: 130 L/h at 3 bar. That's roughly 2.5× the engine's peak consumption and 2.2–2.4× what the injectors can physically pass wide open.

Here's the thing worth understanding: that margin has almost nothing to do with making power. At full throttle, redline, the engine takes about 40% of pump output and 60% goes straight back to the tank. At idle it's more like 1% out, 99% returned. The pump is sized against four other constraints: (1) Regulator stability — a return-style FPR is a spring-loaded diaphragm bypass valve that needs meaningful flow across the seat to sit at a stable position. (2) Service degradation — BMW's condemnation limit is 105 L/h, 81% of nominal, so they built in roughly 20% of allowable wear before the pump is called bad. (3) Voltage sag — these pumps are specified at 12.8 V, and at cold crank you might see 9–10 V. (4) The return loop is the tank's cooling circuit.

Efficiency and heat

Run the numbers on the in-tank pump: 5.2 A at ~13.5 V is about 70 W electrical in. Hydraulic output is Q × Δp = (130 L/h → 3.6×10⁻⁵ m³/s) × 3×10⁵ Pa = 10.8 W. That's about 15% overall efficiency. The stage itself is maybe 20–25%; motor losses and brush drop eat the rest. BMW bought smooth, quiet, damper-free pressure and paid for it with roughly 60 W of waste heat.

Where does that 60 W go? Straight into the fuel — the motor is flooded. At full flow that's about a 1 °C rise per pass, which is nothing. But run the tank turnover: at 130 L/h, a full 55 L tank cycles completely every 25 minutes. With 5 L left, it cycles every 2.3 minutes. The pump's own dissipation alone would raise 5 L of fuel about 25 °C per hour, and that's before the much larger heat load coming back down the return line from a hot engine bay. That's the real explanation for the classic E30 summer symptom — low tank, hot day, hard hot restart or a stumble. Not a weak pump. A tank that's become a small, poorly-cooled reservoir with a heater in it.

Why the architecture changed in 9/1987

Pre-9/87 cars ran two pumps: a low-pressure in-tank pre-supply pump feeding an external main pump under the car. That's not belt-and-braces — it's a necessity. The external Bosch unit is a positive-displacement design mounted above and away from the fuel, and it will not reliably pull a suction lift from a saddle tank without cavitating. The in-tank lift pump exists purely to keep the main pump's inlet flooded.

From 9/87, BMW deleted the external pump and moved to a single in-tank turbine pump doing the whole job. That's a straight cost and NVH win: one pump instead of two, no external plumbing, no exposed pump to corrode, and a submerged turbine is dramatically quieter than an underbody roller-cell. It's the same industry-wide transition that happened everywhere in the late '80s once turbine stages could make 3 bar reliably in a package that fits through a tank aperture. The E30 M3 kept both.

The tank is what shaped the part in the photo

The E30 tank is a saddle straddling the driveshaft tunnel, with the pump in the right lobe. The long tube and the cradle at the bottom exist to put the inlet at the very bottom of a lobe that isn't flat. The swirl pot — a cup around the pump — keeps it submerged during cornering; carbureted 316 tanks don't have one, which is why fitting an injection pump to a 316 tank gives fuel starvation on hard left-handers. Two pipes vs one on the top plate: the 55 L housing carries the rail return, the 63 L tank takes that return elsewhere, and a one-pipe housing physically cannot be used in a 55 L tank. From 1988 the two lobes are split entirely and a siphon jet driven off the return flow transfers fuel from the left lobe to the right, with two level senders averaged for the gauge.

That siphon jet is a nice bit of engineering economy — it's why the generous return flow isn't wasted. The excess fuel the pump is producing but the engine isn't burning is doing useful work as the motive stream for a jet pump on its way home.

If it's the post-9/87 in-tank main pump, the meaningful tests are the three above: 3.0 bar with the vacuum line off, at least 0.875 L in 30 seconds, and residual pressure holding above ~2 bar for several minutes after shutdown. Those three isolate the impeller clearance, the motor, and the check valve respectively.

Prompt 06

the pump is labeled as 12V

Response

That's the nameplate, not a design point — essentially every automotive in-tank pump ever built is marked 12 V, so it doesn't narrow down which E30 pump you have. What would narrow it down are the markings on the outlet cap or stamped into the can: a Bosch 0 580 xxx xxx number, a VDO/Siemens 228-xxx-xxx-xxxZ, or a BMW 16 14 1 xxx xxx. The long alphanumeric on the side of the can in your photo looks like a date/serial code rather than a part number.

But "12 V" is worth pulling on for a different reason, because it's genuinely misleading about how this pump operates. The pump almost never sees 12 V. Running, it sees alternator voltage — 13.5 to 14.2 V. Bosch rates the 070 at 12.8 V. Cranking, with the starter pulling 150+ A, it might see 9–10 V at the terminals. So the label describes the system, and the actual spec is a family of flow-vs-pressure curves at different voltages.

And this pump type is unusually sensitive to that. A permanent-magnet brushed DC motor runs at a speed roughly proportional to applied voltage. A turbine stage's head scales with roughly the square of speed. Put those together: drop from 13.5 V to 11.5 V — about 15% — and shaft speed drops roughly 15%, head capability falls by about 0.85² ≈ 28%, and a pump that deadheaded at 5 bar now deadheads near 3.6 bar.

At 5 bar shutoff, the 3.0 bar operating point sits comfortably down the curve with lots of flow. At 3.6 bar shutoff, 3.0 bar is right up near the knee, and flow collapses far more than 28% — realistically 35–45%. You've gone from 130 L/h to something in the 70–85 L/h range, below BMW's 105 L/h service minimum, with a pump that is mechanically perfectly healthy.

That's the failure mode worth checking before you condemn anything. On a 35-year-old car the usual suspects are the pump ground strap in the tank area, the connector at the hanger studs, the fuel pump relay contacts, and the run of harness back to the tank. It shows up as a lean stumble at high rpm and load — not as a no-start, because 3 bar static is still achievable. Measure it at the pump terminals with the pump running, not at the relay. If you see less than about 12.5 V there with the engine running, the pump isn't your problem yet.

Prompt 07

why did BMW switch from a low pressure sending unit feeding a high pressure pump to a single high pressure pump between the late and early model cars?

Response

One terminology note first: the early car had three things in the tank area that get conflated — the level sending unit (float and resistance card, purely a gauge signal), the in-tank pre-supply pump, and later, the pump housing that carries both. The pre-supply pump wasn't a sender. It was a genuine pump doing genuine work, just at low pressure.

The early architecture wasn't a choice — it was a workaround

The external Bosch pump is a positive-displacement design mounted under the floor, roughly at or above the level of the fuel it's drawing from. That creates a suction-side problem that has a name: net positive suction head. To pull fuel through a long underbody line, the pump has to create a partial vacuum at its inlet. Gasoline has meaningful vapor pressure — summer-blend RVP is around 7 psi, winter blend up to 15, and it climbs steeply with temperature. Drop the local pressure at the pump inlet below the fuel's vapor pressure and the fuel flashes to vapor right there. The pump then tries to compress a gas, loses prime, and delivery collapses.

Every variable works against you: a hot day, a long line, a partially clogged pre-filter, a low tank, a volatile winter blend, and an exhaust running a foot away. That's why BMW put a low-pressure pump in the tank — not to make pressure, but to guarantee the main pump's inlet was always at positive pressure so it could never cavitate. The second pump existed to solve a problem created by where the first pump was mounted.

What changed by 1987

Regenerative turbine pumps existed in the early '80s, but a single-stage turbine is fundamentally head-limited, and the early ones couldn't reliably make 3 bar at useful flow in a can small enough to pass through a tank aperture. What closed the gap over about five years was unglamorous manufacturing progress: better impeller and channel geometry, tighter face-clearance control through grinding and selective assembly, and improved magnetics. Once you could buy a 40 mm × 100 mm pump that made 3.0 bar at 130 L/h all day, the whole external pump became redundant. This wasn't a BMW insight — essentially every European manufacturer made the same transition between roughly 1985 and 1992, because the same Bosch and VDO catalog parts became available to all of them.

What BMW actually gained

  • Vapor lock immunity. Submerge the pump and the NPSH problem doesn't get mitigated, it disappears. The inlet is permanently flooded at tank pressure or better, and the surrounding fuel keeps the pump cool.
  • Noise. An underbody pump is bolted to the body structure and transmits straight up through the floor. A submerged pump sits in rubber isolators, inside a tank, surrounded by liquid. Probably the largest improvement a customer would notice.
  • Cost. One pump instead of two, plus its bracket, heat shield, wiring run, suction line, hard lines, and the clamped joints at each end.
  • Failure modes. The two-pump chain has an ugly property: if the pre-supply pump fails quietly, the main pump starts running dry and cooks itself. One failure took out both. The external pump also lived in the salt and stone-chip environment.
  • Leak and permeation sites. Every hose joint carrying fuel outside the tank can weep, permeate, or be torn open. This mattered increasingly as evaporative emissions rules tightened through the late '80s.
  • Crash exposure. A pressurized pump and lines hanging under the rear floor is a worse rear-impact story than the same hardware inside the tank.

The tank change came with it, and that's not a coincidence

The 9/87 change also split the saddle tank's two lobes and introduced a siphon jet to transfer fuel from the left lobe to the right, with a separate level sender on each side averaged for the gauge. Before that, the lobes were simply joined by a pipe at the bottom with a single sender.

These two changes are coupled. Commit to a single in-tank pump and you must guarantee the pump's lobe never runs dry — there's no longer any backup. A bottom-connecting pipe does that by gravity, but it's a hole low in the tank, which is both a permeation path and a crash liability. A siphon jet needs no low penetration at all: it's a venturi driven by the return flow, and the single high-pressure pump conveniently produces a large surplus of return flow at all times. The excess capacity isn't waste — it's the motive stream for the transfer jet.

Worth noting the 318i kept the older connected-lobe arrangement through 1991, which suggests this was a running change tied to the M20 cars and their tank tooling rather than a clean platform-wide cutover.

Why the M3 kept both: I'd be inferring here rather than stating fact, but the likely reasons are that the E30 M3's fuel system was homologated for Group A in 1986 and changing it would have meant re-certification, and that on an 18,000-unit car the validation cost of qualifying a new in-tank pump for the S14's higher flow wouldn't pay back against carrying over a system that already worked.

[Chart generated: pressure in bar gauge versus angular position from 0° at the inlet to 330° at the outlet. A healthy pump curve rises steeply at first and flattens toward 3.0 bar; a worn pump curve starts with the identical initial slope but flattens far earlier and reaches only about 1.8 bar; a dotted reference line marks the 3.0 bar target.]

A caveat on where these curves come from: this is a physical model, not measured data off a specific pump. I don't have a pressure-tapped test rig or published channel-pressure traces for an E30 pump. But the shape and the endpoints come from real constraints.

The governing balance. Two things happen simultaneously at every point around the ring. The vanes add energy at a nearly constant rate — momentum transfer per pass depends on the velocity difference between the rim and the fuel, which barely changes around the circuit, so the input term is roughly constant per degree. And fuel leaks backward across the impeller face clearance, from wherever it is now to the low-pressure region on the other side of the stripper. That leak is driven by local pressure, so the loss term grows as you go around.

Which gives you dp/dθ = a − bp: a constant gain minus a loss proportional to pressure. That integrates to an exponential approach, p = (a/b)(1 − e^(−bθ)), and that's the shape in the chart. It's steepest at the inlet and progressively flatter toward the outlet, because each successive vane is fighting a bigger back-leak than the one before it.

The asymptote a/b is the deadhead pressure — where gain and leakage exactly cancel. I set the healthy curve's asymptote at 4.5 bar, which lands the 330° outlet at 3.0 bar and is consistent with the E30 test spec that blocking the return should reach about 5 bar. That agreement is a sanity check on the model rather than a coincidence.

Why the worn curve is the interesting one. Both curves start with the same initial slope. That's deliberate and it's the diagnostic point: the impeller is doing identical work in both cases — same rim speed, same vanes, same momentum transfer. What changed is only b, the leakage coefficient, which scales steeply with clearance. Open the face gap from roughly 25 to 50 microns and b roughly triples.

The consequence is that a worn pump is completely normal near the inlet and fails progressively as pressure climbs. It primes normally and sounds normal, it builds static pressure fine at key-on, and it collapses under actual demand. That's why a pressure reading alone can pass a pump that's finished, and why BMW specifies a volume test at pressure — 0.875 L in 30 seconds — rather than just a gauge reading. Static pressure probes the left half of the curve; flow at pressure probes the right half.

Two simplifications worth naming. The real curve is a fine staircase, not smooth — each vane pass is a discrete increment, and with 50 to 100 passes across 330° each step is roughly 30 to 60 mbar. That's the reason the pressure is smooth at the outlet. And the very last few degrees before the stripper are messier than shown, because the pressure gradient across the stripper is at its maximum there.

Prompt 09

can you explain leakage better? where is fluid leaking into/ out of?

Response

[Diagram generated: the ring-shaped pump channel shaded from light at the inlet to dark at the outlet to show pressure building around the circuit, with three numbered arrows showing fuel short-circuiting backward from high-pressure regions to low-pressure ones — past the stripper, inward through the impeller face clearance and back out, and backward over the vane tips.]

"Leakage" is a misleading word here: nothing leaks out of the pump. The can is sealed. Every drop of leakage stays inside and just short-circuits internally, going in circles without doing net work.

1 — Past the stripper. This is the big one, and it's structural rather than a defect. The stripper blocks the channel, but the impeller keeps rotating straight through it. The vane pockets don't empty at the outlet — they carry a slug of 3-bar fuel across the blocked section and dump it into the inlet at 0.1 bar. Plus fluid squeezes through the running clearance between the impeller and the stripper block itself. What makes this severe is that the stripper is the one place where the full pressure rise appears across a gap of a few millimetres.

2 — Inward through the face clearance, then back out. The impeller disc doesn't just have pockets at its rim — it has two large flat faces running 20 to 40 microns from the housing. That gap is a continuous annulus, and it touches every angular position simultaneously. So fuel sitting at 2.8 bar at the 300° mark has a much shorter route home than backing up around the channel: it ducks radially inward into the face gap, slides circumferentially through it, and pops back out into the channel at 60° where the pressure is only 0.8 bar. The whole ring is short-circuited by a thin disc-shaped gap.

3 — Backward over the vane tips. The impeller's outer diameter also runs close to the housing bore. Fuel that should be scooped by the next vane instead slips backward over the top of the rim, sliding down the local pressure gradient within the channel. Smallest of the three.

Why the clearance number is so unforgiving. Flow through a thin gap between two flat surfaces goes as the cube of the gap height — Poiseuille flow, Q ∝ h³Δp / 12μL. Relative leak flow versus face clearance: 25 µm (new) = 1.0×; 35 µm = 2.7×; 50 µm = 8.0×; 75 µm = 27×. Twenty-five microns of wear — a third the thickness of a human hair — multiplies the leak eightfold. This is why the ground faces and the selective assembly bins are the one place the manufacturing cost couldn't be cut.

Two things follow directly. The head-flow curve droops because leakage is pressure-driven: the vanes deliver a roughly constant gross flow, leakage subtracts from it and grows with pressure. At the deadhead point, leak has grown until it exactly equals gross pumping — the vanes are still working just as hard, but 100% of what they move immediately runs back downhill. And leakage is a heat source, not just a flow loss: fuel dropping 3 bar through a 25-micron slot does no useful work, so that pressure energy turns into heat right there in the gap.

Why they don't just seal it: you can't seal against a rotating part without contact, and contact seals mean wear debris, drag, a lubrication requirement, and a service item. The whole economic argument for this pump is that it's sealless, has two journal bushings, and never gets touched for 240,000 km. So the design accepts internal leakage as a permanent tax and manages it purely by making the gaps as small as manufacturing tolerance allows.

Prompt 10

[Attached: excerpt from previous Claude message — "Poiseuille flow: Q ∝ h³Δp / 12μL."]

can you explain this in detail with images showing what it would be like for a part like this?

Response

That formula deserves unpacking, because the exponent is doing all the work and it's genuinely counterintuitive.

What each term means. Q = w · h³ · Δp / (12 μ L). Q is the volumetric leak rate; w is the width of the gap measured across the flow; h is the gap height, the clearance; Δp is the pressure difference driving the leak; μ is the dynamic viscosity of the fuel; L is the length of the gap along the flow direction, the sealing land; and 12 falls out of integrating a parabola — it's not a fudge factor.

Everything except h is linear. Double the width, double the leak. Double the pressure, double the leak. Double the land length, halve the leak. Then h is cubed.

Why cubed: two effects multiply together. One power comes from area — a taller gap is a bigger hole. Two more come from velocity: pressure-driven flow in a narrow slot is held back entirely by viscous drag against the two walls. The fluid touching each wall is stationary (no-slip) and the fluid in the middle is fastest — a parabola. Open the gap and the fluid in the middle is further from both walls, so the velocity gradient it has to sustain is gentler. For the same pressure push, it goes much faster: mean velocity scales with h². Multiply the two: h × h² = h³.

[Diagram generated: two slot cross-sections drawn to the same velocity scale — a 25 µm gap with a small parabolic velocity profile, and a 50 µm gap twice as tall whose parabola is four times longer, so the total flow is eight times greater. Labelled housing above, impeller face below.]

Both panels are drawn to the same velocity scale. The right-hand gap is twice as tall, but its fastest streamline is four times faster. Area under the parabola — which is the flow — goes up eightfold.

Putting real numbers on the pump. I don't have a drawing for this specific pump, so the geometry is representative of a small in-tank turbine stage rather than measured. Consider the single worst leak path: fuel squeezing across the stripper land through the face clearance. Taking w = 6 mm (radial width of the land), L = 8 mm (circumferential length of the stripper block), Δp = 3.0 bar = 3×10⁵ Pa, μ = 4.5×10⁻⁴ Pa·s (gasoline at 20 °C, about half the viscosity of water), and h = 25 µm:

Q = (6×10⁻³)(25×10⁻⁶)³(3×10⁵) / (12 × 4.5×10⁻⁴ × 8×10⁻³) = 6.5×10⁻⁷ m³/s — that's 2.3 L/h per face, or about 4.7 L/h across both, roughly 3.5% of the pump's 130 L/h output through one leak path. Perfectly acceptable. The mean velocity in that gap works out to about 4.3 m/s, and the Reynolds number lands near 360, comfortably laminar, so the formula is in its valid regime.

[Chart generated: flow in L/h versus impeller face clearance in µm, showing delivered flow staying near 130 L/h up to about 30 µm then falling off a cliff, crossing the 105 L/h service minimum near 46 µm and reaching zero around 80 µm, with leakage flow rising as the cube of clearance on the same axes.]

The shape is the point: flat, then a cliff. Nothing happens until about 30 µm, and then delivery falls off a ledge. In this model the pump crosses BMW's 105 L/h service minimum at roughly 46 µm — a little under double the new clearance — and is dead by 80 µm. That's the whole reason those two housing faces get double-disc ground and selectively assembled. The entire useful life of the pump is contained in about 20 microns of wear.

Three honest caveats. First, the impeller face is moving, which helps: pure Poiseuille assumes both walls are stationary, but here one wall is the spinning impeller, dragging fluid along with it — Couette flow superimposed on the pressure-driven backflow. At the stripper, that drag points forward, partly cancelling the leak. The catch is that the Couette contribution scales with h (linear) while the leak scales with h³, so it dominates at tight clearance and becomes irrelevant as the gap opens. Net effect: a new pump seals better than the formula suggests, and the collapse with wear is steeper than the cube law, not gentler.

Second, the cube law expires. Reynolds number in the gap scales with h³ as well. At 25 µm we're at Re ≈ 360, solidly laminar. By roughly 45–50 µm it crosses into transition, and turbulent flow resists more than laminar, so the far right of the chart is pessimistic — real leakage grows closer to h^2.5 up there. Third, I modelled one path; there are three, and the face-clearance short-circuit around the whole ring is arguably larger than the stripper path.

How you'd actually measure it. Feeler gauges are useless at this scale. Either a depth micrometer on the channel recess in each housing half plus a regular micrometer across the impeller thickness, or — easier — a dial indicator: assemble the impeller into one housing half on its shaft, indicate the face, and lift. Total axial float is the sum of both clearances. New should land near 50 µm total. Anything past about 90 µm total and the pump was finished regardless of what the brushes look like.

what is this symbol called?

Response

That's mu — the twelfth letter of the Greek alphabet, lowercase. Pronounced "myoo" in English, "mee" in Greek.

In your formula it's standing for dynamic viscosity, which is far and away its most common use in fluid mechanics. But it's one of the harder-working symbols in engineering: dynamic viscosity (Pa·s) in fluids; coefficient of friction in statics and dynamics, μs and μk; the micro- prefix meaning 10⁻⁶, as in µm; population mean in statistics; permeability in magnetics, μ₀ for free space; and Poisson's ratio in some older texts, though ν (nu) is more standard now.

The micro- prefix is technically a different character in Unicode — µ (U+00B5, "micro sign") versus μ (U+03BC, "Greek small letter mu") — but they render nearly identically and almost nobody distinguishes them in practice.

Worth knowing for a fluids context: μ is dynamic viscosity, and its sibling ν (nu) is kinematic viscosity, where ν = μ/ρ. Getting those two confused is a classic unit disaster, since dynamic is Pa·s and kinematic is m²/s. The Poiseuille equation wants dynamic.

Prompt 14

Can you run some calculations for me on the heating factor of the fuel pump on 10 gallons of gas in winter temps (30 deg f)?

Response

The thermal mass you're heating: 10 US gal = 37.85 L at 30 °F = −1.1 °C. Density ~756 kg/m³ (745 at 15 °C, corrected up ~1.5% for the cold), giving a mass of 28.6 kg. Specific heat ~2.05 kJ/kg·K — gasoline runs 1.9–2.2, and this is the soft number. Thermal mass = 58.6 kJ/K.

So it takes 58.6 kJ to raise the whole tank 1 K, or 32.6 kJ per °F. Another way to hold it: one watt applied for 16.3 hours raises 10 gallons by 1 K.

What the pump puts in. Electrical input at 5.2 A and 13.8 V is about 72 W. Of that, roughly 10.8 W leaves the pump as flow work (130 L/h at 3 bar) and about 60 W is dissipated inside the can — motor I²R, brush drop, bearing friction, and the throttling loss across the leakage gaps. Worth noting where the other 11 W goes: it doesn't escape. The regulator throttles that pressure back to zero and dumps the fuel to the return line, so it also becomes heat, just in the engine bay instead of the tank. Over the whole loop, essentially all 72 W ends up in the gasoline.

Adiabatic rate: 60 W ÷ 58.6 kJ/K = 3.7 K/h = 6.6 °F per hour. That sounds alarming, and it's also wrong, because the tank isn't adiabatic.

[Chart generated: fuel temperature in °F over six hours starting at 30 °F, comparing three cases — no heat loss rising steadily to about 70 °F, idling in still air climbing slowly toward the low 50s, and driving with airflow under the car levelling off near 37 °F within about two hours.]

Why it levels off. The tank is a steel box in a 30 °F airstream, which makes it a reasonably effective radiator. Estimating the wetted area at about 0.6 m² with 10 gallons in it: driving, with underbody airflow, overall U ≈ 25 W/m²K → UA ≈ 15 W/K; idling or parked, still air, U ≈ 5 W/m²K → UA ≈ 3 W/K.

Equilibrium is where heat in equals heat out. Driving: 60 W ÷ 15 W/K = 4 K rise, settling near 37 °F, with a 65-minute time constant. Idling: 60 W ÷ 3 W/K = 20 K rise, settling near 66 °F, with a 5.4-hour time constant.

So the practical answer: driving in 30 °F weather, the pump alone warms 10 gallons by about 7 °F, and it gets there in roughly two hours. It cannot run away, because the loss term grows with the temperature difference. The idling case is a bound rather than a real scenario — nobody idles for five hours — but it shows what happens when you remove the airflow.

The pump is not the main heat source. At 130 L/h the mass flow through the loop is 0.0273 kg/s, so every 1 K the fuel picks up crossing the engine bay = 56 W into the loop. The pump's entire 60 W contribution is equivalent to the fuel warming by 1.1 K in the rail. If the fuel gains even 10 K passing over a warm engine, that's 560 W — nearly ten times what the pump contributes. In winter this is partly offset, because the return line runs under the car in 30 °F air and sheds some of it on the way back. But directionally the conclusion holds: the pump is a minor contributor, and the tank temperature you actually observe is set by the engine bay and by ambient, not by the pump.

Two cold-weather effects worth knowing. The pump works better cold: gasoline viscosity at −1 °C is around 0.65 mPa·s versus 0.45 at 20 °C, and since Q ∝ 1/μ that thicker fuel cuts internal leakage by roughly 30%. A marginal pump will often make spec on a cold morning and fail it in July, so test it warm. And everything is well mixed: at 130 L/h, all 37.85 L passes through the pump every 17.5 minutes — no stratification, no local hot spot.

And the obvious inversion of the summer problem: at 30 °F, gasoline's vapor pressure is far below anything that causes trouble, so all the vapor-lock and hot-restart concerns from the earlier discussion simply don't apply. Winter is when this pump has the easiest life it will ever have.
